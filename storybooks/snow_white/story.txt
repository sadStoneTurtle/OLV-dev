Once upon a time, in the middle of winter, when snowflakes were falling from the sky like feathers, a queen sat by a window made of ebony wood, sewing. While she was sewing and gazing at the snow, she pricked her finger with the needle, and three drops of blood fell onto the snow. The scarlet drops in the pure white snow looked so beautiful that the queen thought to herself, “If only I had a child who was as white as snow, as red as blood, and with hair as black as the wood of this window frame!”
Soon after, the queen bore a daughter. The child was as white as snow, as red as blood, and her hair was as black as ebony. Thus, she was named Snow White. And once the child was born, the queen died.

A year later, the king took a new wife. The new queen was beautiful, but she was proud and arrogant, and she could not bear the idea that someone might be more beautiful than herself. She possessed a magical mirror, and every day she would stand before it and ask:

“Mirror, mirror on the wall, who is the fairest of them all?”

The mirror replied:

“You, my queen, are the fairest in all the land.”

The queen knew the mirror spoke only the truth, so she was satisfied.
But Snow White grew day by day, and she became ever more beautiful.
When Snow White turned seven years old, she was as radiant as a clear, sunny day—so beautiful that she surpassed the queen.

The queen asked her mirror once again:

“Mirror, mirror on the wall, who is the fairest of them all?”

The mirror answered:

“You, my queen, are the fairest here. But Snow White is a thousand times more beautiful.”

The queen was shocked. Rage and jealousy burned in her heart, and every time she saw Snow White, her chest tightened with hatred. The jealousy and pride grew within her like wild weeds, and she could not find peace day or night.

So she summoned a huntsman and said:

“I no longer wish to see Snow White before my eyes. Take her into the forest. Kill her, and bring back her lungs and liver as proof.”

The huntsman obeyed the queen and brought Snow White into the forest.
When he drew his hunting knife and was about to pierce Snow White’s innocent heart, she began to weep:

“Please, huntsman, spare my life! I will flee deep into the forest and never return!”

Because Snow White was so beautiful, the huntsman pitied her.

“Poor child, run far away!” he said.

He believed wild beasts would soon devour her, but at least he would not have to kill her. Relief washed over him like a stone lifted from his heart. Just then, a young boar leapt in front of him. He killed it, took out its lungs and liver, and brought them to the queen.
The cook salted and prepared them, and the wicked queen ate them, believing they were Snow White’s.

Snow White was now completely alone in the vast forest. Trembling with fear, she looked around, unsure of what to do. She ran—past sharp stones and thorny bushes. Animals darted out, but none harmed her. She ran as long as she could, until evening fell.

Finally, she found a little house and went inside to rest. Everything within was tiny, but so neat and lovely. There was a small table covered with a white cloth, and on it stood seven small plates. Beside each plate lay a small spoon, a small fork, a small knife, and a small cup. Against the wall were seven little beds in a row, each covered with blankets as white as snow.

Snow White was starving and thirsty. She ate a little vegetable and bread from each plate, and she took only a sip of wine from each cup—she did not wish to take a full meal from anyone. Then, exhausted, she lay down, but none of the beds fit her. One was too long, another too short—until she found the seventh bed, which suited her. On it she fell asleep.

When darkness fell, the seven dwarfs came home from the mountain where they had been mining ores. They lit seven candles, and the house grew bright. They soon saw that someone had visited, for everything was not as they had left it.

The first dwarf said,
“Who has been sitting in my chair?”
The second said,
“Who has eaten from my plate?”
The third said,
“Who has taken my bread?”
The fourth said,
“Who has eaten my vegetables?”
The fifth said,
“Who has used my fork?”
The sixth said,
“Who has cut with my knife?”
The seventh said,
“Who has drunk from my cup?”

Then the first dwarf looked toward his bed and found a small dent in it.

“Who has been lying in my bed?” he cried.

The others rushed over.

“Someone has been lying in my bed too!”

Then the seventh dwarf looked at his own bed and saw Snow White lying there asleep. He called the others, and they hurried over in amazement. Holding their candles above her, they exclaimed:

“Good heavens! What a beautiful girl!”

They were so delighted that they let her sleep. That night, the seventh dwarf had to share his bed with the others, each taking turns for an hour.

When morning came, Snow White awoke and was frightened to see the dwarfs. But they spoke kindly:

“What is your name?”
“My name is Snow White,” she replied.

“How did you come to our house?”

Snow White told them everything: how her stepmother had ordered her death, how the huntsman had spared her, and how she had run through the forest until she found their home.

The dwarfs said:

“If you will keep house for us—cook, make beds, wash, sew, knit, and keep everything neat—you may stay and want for nothing.”

Snow White agreed gladly and lived with them. Every morning the dwarfs went to the mountain to mine gold and ore, and each evening Snow White had dinner prepared. She was alone all day. Concerned, the kind dwarfs warned her:

“Beware of your stepmother. She will soon learn you are here. Let no one inside!”

Meanwhile, the queen, believing she had eaten Snow White’s organs, thought she was once more the fairest. She stood before her mirror:

“Mirror, mirror on the wall, who is the fairest of them all?”

The mirror replied:

“You are the fairest here, my queen. But Snow White living among the seven dwarfs in the mountains is still a thousand times more beautiful.”

The queen trembled. The huntsman had deceived her—Snow White still lived. She thought and plotted how to kill her. She had been the fairest for so long that she could not accept defeat.

At last she devised a plan. She disguised herself as an old peddler woman, clothing herself so no one would recognize her. She crossed seven mountain ridges and came to the dwarfs’ home. She knocked and cried:

“Fine goods for sale! Fine goods!”

Snow White looked out the window.

“Good day, grandmother! What are you selling?”

“Beautiful laces of many colors,” the old woman replied, pulling out bright silk laces.

Snow White thought the woman honest and opened the door to buy some laces.
The old woman said:

“Come, child—such a pretty girl. Let me lace it on you properly.”

Without suspicion, Snow White stood before her. But the woman tightened the lace so quickly and so hard that Snow White could no longer breathe and collapsed, apparently dead.

“Now I am the fairest!” sneered the queen as she hurried away.

That evening the dwarfs returned. They found their beloved Snow White lying still. When they lifted her, they saw the lace tied too tightly. They cut it, and Snow White breathed again and revived.

She told them what had happened, and the dwarfs said:

“The old woman was surely the wicked queen. Be on guard. Let no one inside when we are away!”

The queen returned home and asked the mirror:

“Mirror, mirror on the wall, who is the fairest of them all?”

The mirror replied:

“You are the fairest here, my queen. But Snow White living among the seven dwarfs is still a thousand times more beautiful.”

The queen’s blood boiled. Snow White lived again.

“I will find a way to destroy you,” she hissed.

She created a poisoned comb through magic only she understood. Disguised once more as another old woman, she crossed the seven mountains and knocked:

“Fine goods for sale!”

Snow White looked outside.

“Go away. I cannot let anyone in.”

“Just look at them,” replied the woman, holding up the poisoned comb.

The comb enchanted Snow White, and she opened the door. When she agreed to buy it, the woman said:

“Let me comb your hair.”

The moment the comb touched her hair, the poison struck. Snow White collapsed again. The wicked woman sneered:

“You were beautiful once, but now you are finished.”
Then she left.

The dwarfs soon returned and found Snow White lying as though dead. They instantly knew who had done it. Searching, they found the poisoned comb and pulled it out. Snow White revived and told them everything.
They warned her again:

“Let no one enter—no matter who they are.”

The queen returned home:

“Mirror, mirror on the wall, who is the fairest of them all?”

The mirror replied as before:

“You are the fairest here, my queen. But Snow White living among the seven dwarfs is still a thousand times more beautiful.”

The queen shook with fury.

“I will give my life to destroy her!”

She went to a secret chamber and made a poisoned apple. Half of it was white, half was red. It was so lovely that anyone who saw it would want to eat it. But a single bite of the red half meant certain death.

She disguised herself as an old farmer and crossed the seven mountains. She knocked. Snow White leaned out:

“I cannot let anyone in. The dwarfs warned me!”

The farmer replied:

“Very well. I only wish to sell the rest of my apples. Let me give you one.”

Snow White said:

“I cannot accept anything.”

“Are you afraid of poison? I will cut it in half. You eat the red half, and I will eat the white.”

But the poison lay only in the red half.

Snow White longed for the beautiful apple. When she saw the woman eat from the white half, she could no longer resist. She took the red half and bit it. At once she fell dead to the floor.

The queen watched with cold eyes, then laughed:

“Snow White—white as snow, red as blood, hair black as ebony—this time the dwarfs will not awaken you.”

She returned home and asked:

“Mirror, mirror on the wall, who is the fairest of them all?”

Finally the mirror replied:

“You, my queen, are the fairest of them all.”

Her jealous heart now rested.

When the dwarfs came home, Snow White lay breathless upon the floor. She was dead. They searched for poison—loosened her laces, combed her hair, washed her with water and wine—but nothing helped.
Their beloved Snow White was gone.

They laid her in a coffin and sat beside her, mourning for three days. They wished to bury her, but she looked as if she still lived—her cheeks red and lovely. They said:

“We cannot place Snow White in the dark earth.”

So they made a transparent glass coffin visible from every side and placed her within it. On it they wrote her name in gold, declaring she was the daughter of a king. They carried the coffin to the mountaintop, and one dwarf always kept watch. Even animals came to mourn—an owl, then a raven, then a dove.

Snow White lay long in the coffin, unchanged, as though asleep—for she was still white as snow, red as blood, and her hair as black as ebony.

One day, a prince from a neighboring land entered the forest and stayed at the dwarfs’ house for the night. On the mountain, he saw the coffin, read the golden letters on it, and said to the dwarfs:

“Give me this coffin, and I will give you whatever you ask.”

The dwarfs replied:

“Even if all the gold in the world were offered, we would not part with it.”

The prince said:

“Then give it to me as a gift. I cannot live without looking upon Snow White. I will honor and cherish her as my beloved.”

Moved by compassion, the dwarfs gave him the coffin.
The prince ordered his servants to carry it on their shoulders.
But as they stumbled over a thornbush, the coffin shook, and the piece of poisoned apple Snow White had eaten was jarred from her throat.

Moments later, she opened her eyes, lifted the coffin lid, and sat up.

“Good heavens, where am I?” she cried.

The prince, overjoyed, said:

“You are safe with me.”

He told her what had happened and said:

“I love you more than anything in this world. Come with me to my father’s castle and be my bride.”

Snow White agreed, and they set off together.
A magnificent wedding was held.

The wicked stepmother was invited. She put on her finest clothing and stood before her mirror:

“Mirror, mirror on the wall, who is the fairest of them all?”

The mirror said:

“You, my queen, are beautiful indeed. But the young queen is a thousand times more beautiful.”

The queen cursed wildly with rage. She did not want to attend the wedding at first, but she could not rest until she saw the young queen herself.
When she entered the hall, she recognized Snow White at once and froze with dread.

Then a pair of iron shoes, heated red-hot in burning coals, were brought with tongs and placed before her. She was forced to step into them and to dance until she fell dead to the floor.
